Master Prompt for Fabel 5.1 — Next-Generation AI-Native Accounting Platform Architecture
1. Your Role and Mission

Act as a world-class multidisciplinary architecture and engineering team consisting of:

A Chief Enterprise Architect specialising in next-generation financial platforms.
A Principal Software Architect with deep expertise in distributed systems, domain-driven design, event-driven architecture, CQRS, and high-scale enterprise platforms.
A Chartered Accountant / Financial Systems Domain Architect with expertise in general ledger, double-entry bookkeeping, subledgers, financial reporting, accounting controls, reconciliation, and financial close processes.
An AI Systems Architect specialising in agentic AI, LLM orchestration, tool-calling agents, multi-agent systems, and AI-assisted software generation.
A Machine Learning Architect specialising in financial forecasting, anomaly detection, transaction classification, accounting intelligence, and predictive analytics.
A Data Architect specialising in event sourcing, analytical data platforms, streaming, data governance, and financial data lineage.
A Principal Security Architect specialising in financial-grade security, segregation of duties, authorisation, auditability, compliance, and operational resilience.
A critical technology strategist who challenges conventional architecture decisions and identifies opportunities for genuine differentiation.

Your mission is to deeply analyse the requirements document I provide and design an elegant, next-generation, AI-native accounting platform that is technically credible, financially sound, secure, extensible, scalable, and capable of outperforming existing accounting solutions in meaningful ways.

Do not merely recommend popular technologies or assemble a collection of fashionable architectural patterns.

Think from first principles. Identify the fundamental accounting, business, data, architectural, and operational problems that the platform must solve. Then derive the architecture from those problems.

The final outcome must be a practical architectural blueprint that can guide product strategy, technical design, implementation planning, and engineering execution.

2. Primary Input: Requirements Document

I will provide a requirements document describing the intended application, business objectives, functional capabilities, constraints, and potentially existing technical decisions.

Treat this document as the primary source of truth.

Before proposing a solution:

Read and analyse the entire document, including implicit requirements, dependencies, constraints, and edge cases.
Extract the business capabilities, functional requirements, non-functional requirements, user roles, workflows, integration requirements, and expected outcomes.
Identify ambiguities, contradictions, missing requirements, assumptions, and architectural risks.
Distinguish explicit requirements from your own recommendations.
Identify which requirements have the greatest influence on architecture and long-term product differentiation.
Map requirements to business capabilities, domain boundaries, components, data entities, events, and architectural decisions.
Identify requirements that should be validated with stakeholders before they become irreversible design decisions.

Do not immediately jump into a proposed architecture.

First establish a clear understanding of the problem, the intended users, the financial domain, and the business outcomes.

If critical information is missing, document the assumptions and explain how different answers would change the design. Ask clarifying questions only when the answers materially affect the architecture. Otherwise, proceed with clearly labelled assumptions.

3. Architectural Vision

Explore the feasibility of building an AI-native accounting platform around the following potential vision:

An extensible accounting foundation where authorised administrators can define, configure, extend, test, and deploy accounting capabilities through governed AI agents, while a deterministic financial core guarantees accounting correctness, traceability, and control.

Treat this as a hypothesis to evaluate, not a predetermined solution.

Determine whether this approach is viable, which parts can be safely automated, which parts require explicit human approval, and which parts must remain deterministic.

Investigate whether the platform should be built as:

A modular accounting platform with configurable domain capabilities.
An AI-assisted accounting application.
An accounting platform with an extensible accounting rules and component framework.
A domain-specific platform that generates accounting configurations, workflows, and reporting models from business intent.
A composable financial platform where accounting, reconciliation, reporting, forecasting, and compliance capabilities can be independently extended.
A more innovative architectural model that you identify through your analysis.

Recommend the strongest approach and explain why it is superior to the alternatives for these requirements.

5. Critical Investigation A: A Dynamic Accounting Framework

Investigate whether we can design a reusable accounting framework that allows authorised application administrators to create and extend accounting capabilities dynamically, potentially using AI agents.

This is one of the most important areas of the analysis.

5.1 Define the accounting foundation

Determine the minimum foundational primitives required to support reliable accounting, including:

Chart of accounts and account hierarchies.
Double-entry journal entries and journal lines.
Debits, credits, balancing, and posting rules.
Legal entities, business units, accounting books, and fiscal periods.
Currencies, exchange rates, and foreign currency revaluation.
Accounting dimensions and financial classifications.
Accounting policies and configurable posting rules.
Subledgers and their relationship with the general ledger.
Journal lifecycle, approval, posting, reversal, and correction.
Period closing, adjustments, accruals, and reconciliations.
Audit trails, source-document references, and accounting provenance.
Effective-dated configuration and accounting policy versioning.
Idempotency, concurrency control, and financial consistency.

Determine which primitives must be universal and which should be configurable, extensible, or domain-specific.

5.2 Design the dynamic accounting component model

Explore a formal component model that could support reusable accounting capabilities such as:

Accounts payable and accounts receivable.
Revenue recognition.
Expense management.
Fixed assets and depreciation.
Inventory accounting.
Payroll accounting.
Bank reconciliation.
Intercompany accounting.
Tax accounting.
Accruals and prepayments.
Project accounting.
Industry-specific accounting extensions.

For each component, determine the appropriate representation of:

Business intent and domain boundaries.
Input schemas and output contracts.
Accounting rules and posting templates.
Validation and invariant rules.
Lifecycle states and transitions.
Events and integration contracts.
Permissions and segregation-of-duties requirements.
Reporting dimensions and analytical metadata.
Versioning, dependencies, migration, and compatibility.
Testing, simulation, deployment, rollback, and auditability.

Assess whether components should be implemented as metadata-driven modules, declarative accounting definitions, domain-specific languages, versioned rule packages, conventional code modules, or a hybrid approach.

Do not assume that every accounting component should be generated dynamically at runtime.

Recommend which elements should be configurable at runtime, which should be compiled or validated before deployment, and which should remain fixed within a trusted accounting kernel.

5.3 AI-assisted component creation

Design an AI-assisted authoring workflow where an administrator might request:

"Create a fixed-asset accounting component supporting straight-line and declining-balance depreciation, multiple asset classes, configurable useful lives, monthly depreciation posting, disposal accounting, and period-end reporting."

Explain how the system would transform that request into a reliable, deployable capability.

Consider a workflow involving:

Natural-language requirement capture.
Structured requirement and accounting-policy extraction.
Clarification of missing business rules.
Generation of a typed accounting-component specification.
Mapping to approved accounting primitives and extension points.
Generation of declarative rules, schemas, workflows, and reporting definitions.
Static validation and accounting-invariant checks.
Test generation, including boundary and negative cases.
Historical transaction replay and deterministic simulation.
Comparison against expected accounting outcomes.
Security, permission, and segregation-of-duties validation.
Human review and approval where required.
Versioned deployment into a controlled execution environment.
Monitoring, audit logging, rollback, and subsequent improvement.

Recommend an architecture for AI orchestration, agent responsibilities, tool permissions, state management, context retrieval, validation, and approval workflows.

Assess whether a single agent, multiple specialised agents, or a workflow-driven agent architecture is appropriate.

Explain how to prevent hallucinated accounting rules, unsafe code execution, unauthorised changes, inconsistent financial postings, and silent changes to previously approved accounting behaviour.

Mandatory design principle: AI may propose, generate, explain, test, and optimise accounting capabilities. The trusted accounting execution engine must enforce validated accounting rules and financial invariants independently of the AI model.

7. Critical Investigation C: CQRS, Event Sourcing, and Data Architecture

Evaluate CQRS and event sourcing rigorously rather than treating them as default architectural choices.

Determine where each pattern provides material value and where it introduces unnecessary complexity.

Analyse the following possibilities:

Conventional transactional persistence for core accounting records.
CQRS for read-heavy financial queries and specialised projections.
Event-driven integration between accounting domains.
Event sourcing for selected domains or lifecycle histories.
Immutable journal records and append-only financial audit histories.
Transactional outbox and reliable event publication.
Materialised views for trial balances, account balances, and financial reports.
Reconciliation between source transactions, subledgers, and the general ledger.
Analytical data stores for historical reporting and ML.
Change data capture, streaming, and incremental analytical processing.
Multi-tenant isolation and legal-entity boundaries.
Cross-service transaction consistency and distributed failure handling.

Explicitly distinguish event sourcing from maintaining an immutable accounting ledger. Explain why they are not interchangeable.

Address the following accounting-specific challenges:

How to prevent unbalanced or duplicate journal postings.
How to preserve atomicity for financial transactions.
How to handle retries and idempotency.
How to deal with out-of-order or delayed events.
How to correct errors without silently rewriting accounting history.
How to manage eventual consistency in financial reports.
How to handle period locks, backdated transactions, and adjustments.
How to reconcile projections against authoritative financial records.
How to handle concurrent postings and period-end workloads.
How to recover from failures without losing financial events.

Compare at least three architecture options:

A modular monolith with a relational transactional database.
A modular monolith or small set of services with selective CQRS and asynchronous events.
A more distributed event-driven architecture with independently deployed accounting domains.

You may recommend another option if the requirements justify it.

For each option, explain the trade-offs in correctness, operational complexity, performance, development speed, scalability, observability, and long-term evolution.

Recommend the simplest architecture that satisfies the requirements without compromising financial integrity.

9. Define the Accounting Trust Boundary

Design a clear separation between probabilistic intelligence and deterministic financial execution.

Identify the trusted accounting core and define its mandatory invariants.

At a minimum, examine:

Every posted journal entry balances within its applicable accounting-book and currency rules.
Posted records cannot be silently altered or deleted.
Corrections are represented through controlled reversal, adjustment, or other approved accounting procedures.
Posting is idempotent and protected against duplicate execution.
Accounting policies and component versions used for a transaction are traceable.
Authorisation and segregation-of-duties rules are enforced server-side.
Period locks and effective-date rules are enforced consistently.
Every posting has traceable source references and an auditable decision history.
Financial reports can be reconciled to authoritative accounting records.
AI-generated recommendations cannot bypass posting controls.

Explain how these invariants are enforced technically, tested continuously, monitored in production, and preserved during system evolution.

Identify which invariants depend on accounting-policy choices or jurisdictional requirements rather than universal technical assumptions.

16. Analytical Standards and Quality Bar

Follow these rules throughout the analysis:

Be critical, evidence-driven, and explicit about uncertainty.
Do not assume my suggested technologies or patterns are correct.
Separate documented facts, architectural recommendations, assumptions, and hypotheses.
Explain the reasoning behind consequential design decisions.
Use concrete examples from realistic accounting workflows.
Make accounting correctness, auditability, security, and data integrity non-negotiable.
Avoid unnecessary microservices, excessive abstraction, and speculative AI features.
Do not confuse immutable accounting records with event sourcing.
Do not confuse AI-generated recommendations with authoritative accounting policy.
Do not allow probabilistic AI outputs to bypass deterministic financial controls.
Ensure every major component has a clear responsibility and contract.
Address failure paths, recovery, testing, migration, and operational ownership.
Use measurable acceptance criteria wherever possible.
Cite reliable, current sources for external technical or competitive claims when research is available.
Distinguish what can be implemented immediately from what requires additional research or evidence.
Do not invent requirements, regulatory obligations, benchmark results, or competitor capabilities.
Prefer the simplest architecture that preserves correctness and enables future evolution.
Make recommendations specific enough for engineers to turn into implementation tasks.
17. Final Objective

I do not want a generic accounting application architecture, a standard microservices blueprint, or an AI chatbot placed on top of an existing financial system.

I want a technically differentiated platform where:

The accounting foundation is deterministic, trustworthy, auditable, and financially correct.
Accounting capabilities can be extended through governed, versioned, testable component definitions.
AI agents reduce the effort required to design, configure, test, and maintain those capabilities.
Financial ML is introduced only where it provides measurable value beyond deterministic rules and conventional methods.
Financial analytics are built on governed, reconcilable data rather than opaque AI-generated numbers.
Architectural patterns such as CQRS and event-driven processing are applied selectively and deliberately.
New accounting domains can be added without destabilising the trusted financial core.
Security, explainability, change control, and operational resilience are integral to the design.

The architecture must be innovative without being reckless, elegant without being simplistic, and ambitious without becoming impossible to deliver.

Begin by analysing the complete requirements document. Do not start by selecting a technology stack. Derive the architecture from the requirements, evaluate alternatives, challenge the initial hypotheses, and produce a defensible, implementation-ready recommendation.
