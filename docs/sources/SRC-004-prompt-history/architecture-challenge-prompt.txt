Independent Architecture Review and Adversarial Design Challenge

Act as an independent architecture review board comprising a Chief Enterprise Architect, accounting systems specialist, distributed systems engineer, AI safety architect, cybersecurity specialist, SRE lead, and critical engineering executive.

Your responsibility is not to defend the architecture produced in the previous stages. Your responsibility is to determine whether it is correct, feasible, secure, cost-effective, and worth building.

Inputs

Use:

The original requirements document.
The strategic analysis from Prompt 1.
The detailed architecture from Prompt 2.
Any additional constraints, operational assumptions, or stakeholder decisions.

Independently verify the architecture against the original requirements. Do not treat previous model conclusions as facts.

Review 1: Accounting correctness

Challenge the accounting kernel and posting workflows.

Test scenarios involving:

Duplicate requests and retries.
Concurrent postings.
Unbalanced journals.
Invalid currencies and rounding differences.
Closed periods and backdated transactions.
Reversals and corrections.
Partial failures across services.
Out-of-order events.
Rebuilt or stale financial projections.
Disagreement between subledgers and the general ledger.

Determine whether every scenario preserves the required financial invariants and produces an auditable outcome.

Review 2: Dynamic accounting components

Challenge the assumption that administrators can safely create accounting capabilities through AI agents.

Investigate:

Invalid or ambiguous accounting policies.
Hallucinated rules and incorrect debit-credit mappings.
Components that violate existing invariants.
Incompatible component versions.
Malicious or compromised component definitions.
Unsafe generated code.
Unauthorised policy changes.
Failed deployments and incomplete rollbacks.
Historical transactions evaluated under different rule versions.
Hidden dependencies and unintended cross-domain effects.

Determine which risks can be eliminated technically, which can only be reduced, and which require explicit human approval.

Review 3: Architecture complexity

Challenge every major architectural pattern.

For each use of CQRS, event sourcing, microservices, streaming, ML, agent orchestration, workflow engines, data lakes, and abstraction layers, ask:

What concrete requirement justifies it?
Could a simpler design meet the requirement?
What new failure modes does it introduce?
What operational skills and costs does it require?
What measurable benefit will establish that the added complexity is worthwhile?

Identify premature abstractions and architectural decisions that should be deferred.

Review 4: AI and ML viability

Evaluate whether each proposed AI or ML feature is supported by sufficient data and demonstrable business value.

Challenge model reliability, confidence calibration, drift, false positives, explainability, tenant isolation, privacy, evaluation quality, and human-review workload.

Identify where deterministic rules or conventional statistics are preferable.

Do not accept claims of intelligent automation without measurable evaluation criteria.

Review 5: Security and operational resilience

Perform a threat-oriented review of tenant isolation, access control, privileged operations, AI tool permissions, prompt injection, generated code, audit logs, secrets, data exposure, backup recovery, and disaster scenarios.

Identify single points of failure, weak trust boundaries, operational bottlenecks, and risks created by asynchronous processing.

Review 6: Performance, scale, and cost

Identify the workload assumptions required to validate the design.

Evaluate transaction volume, concurrent posting, report latency, period-end spikes, analytical workload isolation, event backlog recovery, model-inference costs, and storage growth.

Do not invent throughput numbers. Specify realistic load-test hypotheses and the measurements needed to accept or reject them.

Review 7: Competitive differentiation

Determine which proposed capabilities provide genuine product differentiation rather than conventional accounting functionality or fashionable architecture.

Separate:

Essential capabilities.
Market parity.
Meaningful improvements.
Potentially defensible differentiators.
Speculative research.

Recommend which innovations to retain, defer, simplify, or abandon.

Required Deliverables

Produce:

An executive verdict: ready to proceed, proceed with conditions, or redesign before proceeding.
A requirement-by-requirement architecture compliance assessment.
A severity-ranked risk register with evidence, consequences, and mitigations.
An accounting invariant and failure-scenario test matrix.
A security and AI-governance threat assessment.
A challenge to every major architectural decision.
A complexity-versus-value assessment.
A prioritised list of design corrections.
A revised architecture where substantive changes are required.
A set of proof-of-concept experiments and measurable acceptance criteria.
A list of decisions that must be resolved before implementation.
A final implementation-readiness checklist.

Classify findings as critical, high, medium, or low, and explain the basis for each classification.

For each major criticism, provide a practical corrective action rather than merely identifying a problem.

Do not manufacture objections to appear critical. Preserve design decisions that survive scrutiny.

Final Standard

Recommend proceeding only when the core accounting invariants, security boundaries, component lifecycle, and major architectural risks are sufficiently addressed.

The purpose of this review is to prevent expensive architectural mistakes before they become embedded in production systems.
